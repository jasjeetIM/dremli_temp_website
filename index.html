A prototype is easy. Production is not.

Calling a model API is rarely the difficult part. The challenge is making AI perform reliably on a specific workflow, with specific data, under real operational constraints.

Which model should we use?

Benchmark frontier, open-weight, and specialized models on the actual task.

How do we know it works?

Build evaluations from historical cases, expert judgment, known failure modes, and business requirements.

Should we fine-tune?

Test whether prompting, retrieval, fine-tuning, distillation, or another architecture actually improves performance.

How should an agent act?

Define tools, permissions, state, recovery, escalation, and task-level success criteria.

How do we control cost and latency?

Measure quality against inference cost and latency, then route work to the appropriate model or system.

What happens after deployment?

Use production failures and human corrections to expand evaluations and improve the system.

From business problem to measured system.

Industrial equipment support

A manufacturer has service manuals, sensor data, repair histories, parts records, and technician notes. It wants AI to help diagnose equipment failures.

Evaluation targets

Before deployment, define the performance the system must meet.

Illustrative evaluation targets for industrial equipment support
MeasureIllustrative target
Diagnosis accuracy≥ 90%
Top-3 recall≥ 97%
Unsupported claims< 0.5%
P95 latency< 3 sec
Average inference cost< $0.10

These are illustrative engineering targets, not reported Dremli results.

A frontier model may produce the best accuracy but be too expensive for every request. A smaller specialized model may handle routine cases, while harder cases route to a frontier model and safety-critical cases go to a human.

The right architecture depends on the workload, risk, and economics.

Architecture should follow measurement, not preference.

Model-agnostic by design.

Evals before optimization.

If we cannot measure the task, we cannot reliably improve it.

Use the simplest system that works.

The right answer may be a frontier model, a specialized smaller model, retrieval, deterministic software, or a combination.

Optimize the complete system.

Quality, reliability, latency, cost, security, maintainability, and operational risk all matter.

Learn from production.

Failures and human corrections should become evaluation cases, regression tests, and training data where appropriate.

Technical decisions should be supported by evidence.

We test models and architectures against the client's actual workload, document the tradeoffs, and make clear recommendations about what to build and why.

Where useful, that work includes model benchmarks, architecture experiments, evaluation design, and cost-performance analysis.
